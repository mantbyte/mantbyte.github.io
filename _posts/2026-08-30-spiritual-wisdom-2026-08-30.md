In the *Lotus Sutra*, one of the most revered texts of Mahayana Buddhism, there is a story of a man who visits the home of a close friend. They feast and drink until the man falls into a deep, unconscious sleep. Before the host has to depart on urgent business, he sews a priceless, wish-fulfilling gem into the lining of his sleeping friend's robe, wanting to ensure his future prosperity.

The man eventually wakes up, unaware of the treasure he carries. He travels from city to city, struggling to find work, living in rags, and constantly worrying about his next meal. He spends years in a state of "spiritual poverty," exhausted by the mere effort of survival. It is only much later, when he encounters his old friend again, that the secret is revealed. "Why have you lived like a beggar?" the friend asks, pointing to the hem of the man's own garment. "You have been wealthy this entire time."

This parable illustrates the concept of *Tathagatagarbha*, or the "Buddha-nature"—the idea that every individual possesses an innate, incorruptible wisdom and value. We are like the traveler, wandering through the "poverty" of our anxieties, insecurities, and the relentless pursuit of external success, completely oblivious to the "gem" of our own inherent worth. We act as if we are empty vessels that must be filled with achievements or possessions, when in reality, we are already complete.

In the modern world, we often tie our value to our productivity, our social status, or the digital approval of strangers. We treat ourselves as projects to be "fixed" or "upgraded," operating under the assumption that we are fundamentally lacking. The parable of the hidden gem invites us to shift our perspective: we are not seeking to *become* something valuable; we are seeking to *recognize* the value that was never absent. 

Applying this means practicing a form of "radical self-recollection." When the world tells us we are not enough, we can pause and remember the gem in the hem. Our peace does not need to be manufactured; it only needs to be uncovered. By shifting from a mindset of "acquisition" to one of "discovery," we stop wandering as beggars and begin to live from a place of original abundance.
