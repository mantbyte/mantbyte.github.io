In ancient Indian folklore, there is a tale of a weary traveler who unwittingly wandered into the celestial realm of Indra. Parched, exhausted, and bewildered by the road, he collapsed on a patch of emerald moss beneath the shade of a magnificent, ancient tree. 

He did not know that he had laid down beneath the branches of the *Kalpavriksha*—the mythic, wish-fulfilling tree. 

Lying against its trunk, his stomach growled, and he whispered to the wind, “I am so famished. If only a feast were spread before me.” 

Before the words had fully evaporated, the air shimmered. Silver platters laden with fragrant saffron rice, steaming stews, and delicate sweets materialized out of empty space. Stunned but too hungry to question his fortune, the traveler ate ravenously. When his appetite was sated, a deep thirst arose. “Ah, if only I had something cool and fragrant to drink,” he murmured. Instantly, crystal goblets filled with spiced nectars and pure mountain water rested beside his hand.

Delighted and heavy with satisfaction, drowsiness overcame him. “If only I had a soft bed upon which to rest my bones,” he thought. In a heartbeat, a low wooden cot draped in cool silk appeared. The traveler climbed onto the cushions, sinking into luxurious comfort.

Yet as he lay there in total ease, a creeping sense of unease began to stir within his mind. The rational intellect, accustomed to the friction, struggle, and conditional nature of the world, rebelled against unearned perfection. 

*This makes no sense,* the man reasoned. *Food out of thin air? A bed in the middle of nowhere? What kind of dark forest is this? There must be demons lurking here.* 

No sooner had the thought taken shape than ferocious spirits with fiery eyes and gnashing fangs sprang from the shadows of the forest. Paralyzed with horror, his racing heart struck the final blow: *They are going to slaughter me! A tiger will come and tear me limb from limb!* 

A great roar ripped through the clearing. A ravenous beast leaped from the undergrowth, and the man was devoured.

***

The parable of the Kalpavriksha is not a children's cautionary tale about magical beings; it is a profound psychological diagnostic of the human mind. The wish-fulfilling tree is our own consciousness. 

Consciousness possesses an extraordinary, generative potency. It does not judge the emotional quality or the wisdom of the commands we feed it; it simply builds whatever reality our dominant attention demands. When the mind focuses on lack, nourishment arrives as a fleeting relief; but when the mind, left unmonitored, begins to manufacture suspicion, catastrophizing, and terror, consciousness dutifully materializes those specters into our lived reality.

In our contemporary world, many of us live precisely beneath the branches of this tree. By the objective standards of our ancestors, the modern citizen commands wonders that would resemble sorcery: climate-controlled rooms, an abundance of food at our fingertips, instant access to all human literature, and comforts once reserved for kings. 

Yet, having settled into our beds of silk, what does the untrained intellect do? It immediately conjures the demons.

We sit in safety and manifest phantom catastrophes: the terrifying scenarios of future financial ruin, the imagined judgments of colleagues, the doomscrolling that conjures global collapse into the quiet sanctity of our living rooms. We invent the tiger before it ever exists in the thicket, and our nervous systems pay the physiological price of being torn apart every single day.

The tragedy of the traveler was not that the tree granted his wishes; it was that he did not realize he was the author of the nightmare. He believed the demons came from the forest, blind to the fact that they were born in the theater of his own unanchored thoughts.

To navigate modern existence with sovereignty requires recognizing the nature of the tree under which we sit. We cannot afford the luxury of undisciplined, catastrophic imagination. Every anxious narrative, every paranoid projection, and every worst-case scenario we obsessively rehearse is an invocation made beneath the branches.

True freedom begins with the quiet pause between perception and interpretation. When unease arises in the midst of comfort, we must look not to the shadowy forest, but directly at the thought factory within. Before you imagine the tiger, remember where you are sitting—and let the empty sky remain empty.
