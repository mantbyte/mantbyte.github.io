In the 18th century, the Zen master Hakuin Ekaku was revered by his community as a man of exceptional purity. This reputation was suddenly shattered when the daughter of a local food seller became pregnant. Under pressure from her angry parents to name the father, she eventually pointed toward the temple and named Hakuin.

When the parents confronted the master with accusations and insults, Hakuin did not protest his innocence or offer an alibi. He simply looked at them and asked, "Is that so?"

After the child was born, the parents brought the infant to Hakuin, demanding he take responsibility for the consequence of his 'sin.' Though his reputation was ruined and the townspeople now treated him with scorn, Hakuin accepted the child. He spent the next year meticulously caring for the infant, begging for milk and supplies from neighbors who once bowed to him but now spat in his path. 

Eventually, the young mother could no longer endure the guilt. She confessed to her parents that the real father was a young man who worked in the fish market. Horrified by their mistake, the parents rushed to Hakuin’s hut, prostrating themselves and begging for forgiveness. They explained the truth and asked to take the child back. Hakuin’s only response, as he gently handed the baby over, was, "Is that so?"

### The Modern Application: Reclaiming the Energy of Defense

We live in an age of the 'reputation economy.' Through social media and constant digital connectivity, we are perpetually engaged in the curation and defense of our public image. When we are misunderstood, misquoted, or unfairly judged, our immediate instinct is to launch a vigorous defense. We feel that if we do not 'set the record straight,' our very identity is at risk. This constant state of defensiveness is exhausting; it tethers our inner peace to the fickle opinions of others.

Hakuin’s "Is that so?" is a masterclass in psychological sovereignty. It suggests that our true value is not a consensus reached by the public, but an internal reality. When we stop struggling to control the shadows we cast in the minds of others, we reclaim a massive amount of mental energy. 

Practicing this doesn't mean we never speak the truth, but it means we stop being *dependent* on others believing it. To be able to stand in the center of a storm of gossip or false judgment and remain unmoved is the ultimate form of power. By detaching our ego from our reputation, we find that the 'self' we were so desperate to protect was never actually in danger. Like Hakuin, we can focus on the task at hand—in his case, caring for a child—without being distracted by the noise of the world’s approval or disapproval.
