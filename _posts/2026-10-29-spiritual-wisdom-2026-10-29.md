To the northeast of the ancient Chinese state of Lu stood Mount Niu, or Ox Mountain. Travelers beholding its windswept ridges saw only scarred earth, jagged rocks, and dust. It appeared so desolate that onlookers assumed the mountain was born barren—a wasteland incapable of harboring life.

In the *Mengzi*, the philosopher Mencius stops to correct this illusion with a quiet, arresting observation. Ox Mountain, he explains, was once crowned with lush, towering forests of jade foliage and emerald pines. But because it stood close to the outskirts of a booming metropolis, woodcutters arrived with axes and hatchets day after day, year after year, felling the trees for timber and firewood. How could any woodland preserve its splendor against such relentless harvest?

Even so, Mencius notes, the natural vitality of the mountain did not perish immediately. Each evening, as dusk fell and the woodcutters returned home, the mountain rested under the stars. Bathed in the moist air and gentle dew of the night, slender green sprouts pushed their way through the battered soil, striving to begin anew. But with dawn came herds of cattle, sheep, and goats, devouring the fragile shoots before they could take root. Stripped bare again and again, the soil finally eroded, leaving nothing but naked stone.

Seeing it in this wretched state, people shook their heads and said, *“This mountain has always lacked timber; such is its nature.”*

### The Clearcutting of the Human Heart

For Mencius, Ox Mountain is not merely a lesson in ecology; it is an urgent diagnostic mirror held up to human consciousness. 

We often look upon our own minds—exhausted, anxious, cynical, or callous—and fall into the same trap as the onlookers at Ox Mountain. We assume that burnout, irritability, and inner desolation are our baseline condition. We whisper to ourselves that we are fundamentally broken, naturally deficient in discipline, or inherently cold toward the world.

Mencius reminds us that human nature possesses an innate sprout of goodness, wonder, and profound equanimity. We are designed to be lush, generous, and clear. If we find ourselves barren, it is not because we were formed without timber, but because our inner landscape is being clearcut every single day.

Consider the axes that strike at your consciousness from dawn to dusk: the endless demands of professional performance, the abrasive static of algorithmic outrage, the notifications slicing into your attention, and the friction of petty social comparisons. By evening, our psychic forest has been chopped down to the roots.

### The Mystery of the “Night Spirit”

Yet the most poignant element of Mencius’s teaching is what he calls *ye qi*—the “night air” or “night spirit.” 

When the noise of the world recedes, consciousness naturally seeks its own restoration. During the quiet hours of twilight, deep sleep, and solitude, the soul releases its defensive armor. In this nocturnal sanctuary, the moisture of our deeper nature stirs. We wake up with delicate shoots of clarity: a momentary impulse toward forgiveness, a fresh burst of creative wonder, a subtle yearning for peace.

Tragically, modern life gives these shoots no room to mature. Before our feet even touch the floor in the morning, we reach for our phones. We let the herds of cattle and goats—the incoming demands, the morning news, the sudden deluge of worldly anxieties—graze upon the tender growth of the night spirit before the day has even properly begun. Within minutes, the fragile shoots are obliterated, leaving the mind as dry and rocky as it was the day before.

When this cycle repeats across months and years, we lose touch with who we were beneath the exhaustion. We mistake the barrenness of our over-grazed environment for the nature of our soul.

### Protecting the Shoots

To apply Mencius’s wisdom today is to realize that self-transformation is rarely a matter of forcing yourself to become someone grander; it is a discipline of radical protection.

You do not need to construct a new soul. You simply need to stop the perpetual hacking at the one you have.

1. **Acknowledge the Woodcutters:** Begin by identifying the specific axes that harvest your peace. Which relationships drain your emotional reservoir without replenishing it? Which digital habits clearcut your focus? You cannot heal your mountain while willingly sharpening the woodsman’s blade.

2. **Sanctify the Night Spirit:** Guard the boundaries of your twilight and dawn. If you allow the noise of the world to be the last thing you ingest before sleep and the first thing you graze upon at waking, your inner seedlings will never survive. Create a buffer of quiet. Let your mind rest under the dew of silent contemplation, reading, or undistracted rest.

3. **Do Not Judge the Mountain by the Rubble:** In moments when you feel numb, cynical, or depleted, remember Ox Mountain. Your present fatigue is not a character flaw; it is an ecological consequence. The timber remains latent in the soil, waiting only for an armistice.

Give the mountain time, withdraw the axes, fence out the grazing herds, and trust the regenerative power that lives beneath the stones. Given sanctuary, the bare rock will inevitably turn green once more.
