In the fifth chapter of the *Brihadaranyaka Upanishad*, one of the oldest and most revered philosophical texts of India, there unfolds a striking encounter between the creator-teacher, Prajapati, and his three classes of pupils: gods (*devas*), human beings (*manushyas*), and demons (*asuras*).

Having lived a period of disciplined discipleship, the three groups approached Prajapati one by one to ask for their final, parting instruction before entering the world.

First came the gods. Prajapati looked upon them and uttered a single syllable: *"Da."* He then asked, *"Have you understood?"*

The gods replied, *"Yes, we have understood. You say to us, 'Damyata'—restrain yourselves."*
Prajapati nodded and said, *"Yes, you have understood."*

Next came the humans. Prajapati looked at them and spoke the exact same syllable: *"Da."* He asked, *"Have you understood?"*

The humans replied, *"Yes, we have understood. You say to us, 'Datta'—give."*
Prajapati nodded and said, *"Yes, you have understood."*

Finally, the demons came before the teacher. Prajapati spoke the very same syllable: *"Da."* He asked, *"Have you understood?"*

The demons answered, *"Yes, we have understood. You say to us, 'Dayadhvam'—be compassionate."*
Prajapati smiled and replied, *"Yes, you have understood."*

The Upanishad notes that to this day, when the thunder clouds roar in the sky with the sound *"Da-Da-Da,"* the cosmos is repeating these three vital commands: *Damyata* (Control yourself), *Datta* (Give), and *Dayadhvam* (Have compassion).

---

### The Anatomy of the Three Afflictions

Why did one single sound convey three entirely different obligations? The Upanishadic sages recognized that these three categories—gods, humans, and demons—are not separate mythological beings living in distant realms. Rather, they are three archetypal tendencies residing within every human heart.

1. **The God-nature (Prone to Hedonism):** The gods live in abundance, beauty, and celestial privilege. Their vulnerability is not malice or greed, but unchecked indulgence and distraction. For those in positions of ease, comfort, and success, the necessary counterweight is **Damyata**—mastery over desires and intentional restraint.

2. **The Human-nature (Prone to Acquisitiveness):** Mortals are constantly haunted by the ghost of scarcity. Aware of their frailties and the uncertainty of tomorrow, humans tend to hoard, cling, and measure everything transactionally. The antidote to this anxiety-driven hoarding is **Datta**—generosity, charity, and letting go of accumulated wealth and emotional energy.

3. **The Demon-nature (Prone to Aggression):** The *asuras* are defined by wrath, egoic dominance, and hardness of heart. When thwarted, they seek to harm or conquer. The antidote to this destructive impulse is **Dayadhvam**—empathy, active tenderness, and recognizing the vulnerability in others.

### Applying the Three 'Da's to Modern Life

In our hyper-connected, fast-paced world, we regularly rotate between being the god, the human, and the demon.

* **When you are the 'God':** You find yourself awash in dopamine—endlessly scrolling luxuries, celebrating a career peak, or insulated by comfort. In these moments, wisdom does not demand that you strive for more; it whispers *Damyata*. Step back, embrace voluntary simplicity, curb the impulse to consume endlessly, and guard your sovereignty of mind.

* **When you are the 'Human':** You feel the pinch of insecurity. You begin calculating what people owe you, guarding your time jealously, or fearing there isn't enough success to go around. In these moments, the prescription is *Datta*. Disrupt the illusion of scarcity by offering your resources, your attention, or your praise to someone else without expecting a return.

* **When you are the 'Demon':** You feel bitter, slighted, or judgmental. You find yourself crafting sharp retorts online, holding grudges, or viewing opponents with cold contempt. In these moments, the cure is *Dayadhvam*. Soften the posture. Recognize that behind hostility almost always lies an unacknowledged wound.

Wisdom is rarely a rigid rulebook applied uniformly to every situation. Like Prajapati's single thunderous note, real philosophy acts as an intelligent mirror: it asks you to look honestly at which impulse is dominating your life right now, and to apply precisely the medicine required to bring the soul back into harmony.
