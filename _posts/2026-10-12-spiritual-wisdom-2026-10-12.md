In the fifteenth chapter of his *Enchiridion*, the Stoic philosopher and former slave Epictetus offered a concise, luminous portrait of how a wise soul moves through existence. 

"Remember that you ought to behave in life as you would at a banquet," he taught his students. "A dish is brought round to you: stretch out your hand and take a polite portion. Does it pass by you? Do not stop it. Has it not yet come to you? Do not project your desire toward it afar off, but wait until it arrives in front of you."

At first glance, Epictetus’s banquet seems like a primer on table manners. Yet underneath the etiquette lies a profound psychology of desire, surrender, and inner sovereignty.

Consider the mechanics of the ancient feast. At a great dinner, servants carry platters through a hall of guests. Some platters hold wine, others seasoned meats, others bitter herbs, and some pass quickly without stopping. A banquet is inherently communal, orchestrated by a host whose designs are beyond the control of any individual diner. 

Epictetus identifies three habitual errors people make at this cosmic table:

1. **The Grab**: When a desirable dish—wealth, affection, recognition, or ease—arrives, we often pile our plates high with frantic greed, fearing we will never taste it again. In doing so, we become gluttons of circumstance, losing our composure.
2. **The Cling**: When the platter is lifted away and moves down the table, our instinct is to snatch at the server’s sleeve, demanding that the course linger. We refuse to accept that transient things must depart by their very nature.
3. **The Craving**: Worst of all is our habit of peering down the banquet hall, obsessing over what someone else is currently being served, stretching our necks and projecting our appetite across the room for something that has not even reached our seat.

Epictetus reminds us that life distributes its courses—reputation, health, companionship, hardship, and loss—according to laws we did not author. To rage because a particular dish passed you by is as absurd as screaming at a server who carried the roast to the next table. You are not the master of the feast; you are a guest.

Yet Epictetus adds a striking final thought to his metaphor. If you can handle the passing dishes with moderation and release, you are a worthy guest. But if you can look at the fine dishes laid directly before you and decline them altogether—indifferent to their fleeting sensory appeal—you cease to be merely a guest at the banquet; you become a peer of the gods.

### The Modern Buffet and the Agony of Comparison

In our contemporary world, Epictetus’s metaphor feels sharper than ever. We live in an era that functions as an infinite, hyper-visible digital banquet. Social media places the platters of millions of strangers directly before our eyes, allowing us to see every feast, promotion, romantic milestone, and exotic vacation enjoyed by others.

The modern mind spends its days in an agony of "projecting desire afar off." We are tortured by dishes that were never placed at our table, ignoring the simple, nourishing bread resting right before us. When good fortune does visit us, we devour it with anxious speed, terrified that the algorithm of life will withdraw it; when it leaves, we fall into despondency, nursing grievances against the world.

To practice Epictetus’s banquet rule today requires cultivating three quiet disciplines:

* **Courteous Reception**: When good fortune, joy, or love appears, welcome it with full attention and gratitude. Do not taint the moment with guilt or frantic grasping. Take your polite share.
* **Graceful Release**: When a phase ends—a career title fades, a relationship dissolves, or youth yields to age—do not grab the server’s arm. Let the tray pass with dignity, knowing that what enters the room must eventually leave it.
* **Stillness in Anticipation**: Stop straining your neck to see what life is giving to those ten seats away from you. Covetous anticipation robs the present of its dignity.

True freedom does not consist of owning the banquet hall or dictating the evening’s menu. It lies in conducting oneself with such self-contained nobility that whether the tray is full, passing, or absent, the serenity of the diner remains undisturbed.
