In the treasury of Zen wisdom, there is perhaps no story that captures the paradox of human existence more poignantly than the parable of the man and the strawberry. The story describes a traveler walking across a vast field who suddenly encounters a ravenous tiger. The man flees, but he soon reaches the edge of a sheer precipice. With the tiger closing in behind him, he spots a wild vine hanging over the cliff's edge and scrambles down to escape.

As he hangs suspended over the abyss, he looks down only to see a second tiger waiting at the bottom to devour him. To make matters worse, two mice—one white and one black—emerge from a crevice and begin to gnaw steadily at the vine that holds him. At that moment, the man notices a single, luscious wild strawberry growing from the cliffside within arm's reach. Holding onto the vine with one hand, he plucks the strawberry with the other and eats it. He exclaims to the empty air, 'How sweet it is!'

This story serves as a profound map of the human condition. The tiger behind us represents our past and the inevitability of birth, which has already set us on this journey. The tiger below represents our future and the certainty of our end. The black and white mice are the relentless cycles of night and day—time itself—which nibbles away at the 'vine' of our lifespan. We are, in a very real sense, always suspended in a state of precariousness, caught between what has been and what will be, while time slowly erodes our security.

In modern life, we often spend our energy trying to kill the tigers or scare away the mice. We obsess over safety, insurance, and legacy, or we become paralyzed by the 'gnawing' of our busy schedules. We postpone our joy until the tigers are gone, not realizing that the tigers are an inherent part of the landscape. The Zen teaching suggests that the strawberry—the immediate, sensory, and beautiful reality of the present moment—is not a distraction from our problems, but the very point of being alive.

Applying this today means practicing 'radical appreciation.' It is the ability to fully taste a cup of coffee, feel the warmth of the sun, or listen intently to a friend's voice, even while knowing that work is stressful, the future is uncertain, and our time is finite. To eat the strawberry is to refuse to let the 'tigers' of anxiety and the 'mice' of time rob us of the only thing we ever truly possess: the sweetness of the Now.
