Two Buddhist monks, Tanzan and Ekido, were once traveling along a muddy country road after days of relentless torrential rain. As they rounded a bend, they came upon an intersection submerged in thick, impassable sludge. Standing on the verge was a young woman in an exquisite silk kimono, unable to cross without ruining her delicate garments.

Without a moment's hesitation, Tanzan walked forward, scooped the woman up in his arms, carried her across the mud, and set her down gently on the dry ground. She bowed in gratitude, and Tanzan simply continued walking.

Ekido walked beside him in stunned silence. For a monk under strict monastic vows, touching a woman—let alone carrying one in one’s arms—was a grave violation of ascetic conduct. An hour passed, then two, then three. The silence between the two travelers grew heavier with each mile. Ekido was visibly agitated, his brow furrowed, his breathing shallow, replaying the scandalous incident over and over in his mind.

Finally, as evening fell and they arrived at a lodging temple, Ekido could bear the pressure no longer. He burst out in righteous fury: 

*"How could you do that? We are monks! We took solemn vows never to go near women, let alone touch them. What you did was utterly improper and dangerous!"*

Tanzan turned to his companion with calm eyes, smiled warmly, and replied: 

*"I put that woman down by the river hours ago. Why are you still carrying her?"*

### The Anatomy of the Mental Pack Mule

This classic Zen encounter cuts straight to the core of human psychological suffering. It reveals the vast gulf between physical action in the present moment and the phantom weight of mental rumination.

Tanzan acted with spontaneous compassion. Faced with a concrete situation, he responded practically, accomplished the task, and dropped it completely the second his feet touched dry ground. His mind was clean, lucid, and unburdened because he did not drag the event into the future.

Ekido, by contrast, had refused to touch the woman physically, yet he had been carrying her for hours. In his mind, he carried not just her, but his rigid dogmas, his judgments, his moral outrage, and an imaginary trial of his companion. He transformed a five-second act of kindness into an entire afternoon of self-inflicted torment.

We often believe that our stress comes from what happens to us. In reality, suffering is rarely generated by the event itself; it is manufactured by our refusal to set it down once it is over.

### Modern Riverbanks: The Phantom Weight We Carry

In our modern landscape, we rarely carry travelers across muddy roads, but we carry countless invisible ghosts:

1. **The 9:00 AM Email at Dinner:** A sharp or passive-aggressive message arrives in your inbox in the morning. The exchange concludes within fifteen minutes. Yet at 8:00 PM, while sitting across from your family, your mind is still drafting defensive rebuttals, dissecting every word, and stewing in indignation. The email was set down hours ago; you are still carrying the sender.
2. **The Rehearsal of Grievance:** When someone cuts us off in traffic or makes a careless remark, the interaction lasts mere seconds. Yet for the rest of the day, our heart rate spikes as we replay the encounter, inventing sharper retorts and feeding our resentment. We poison our present moment to punish an offense that has already ceased to exist.
3. **The Guilt of Past Mistakes:** We stumble, make an error in judgment, or face an awkward social encounter. Rather than adjusting our behavior and moving on, we drag the corpse of our blunder through days, weeks, or years of remorse.

### The Practice of Immediate Release

To live with the clarity of Tanzan requires cultivating what Zen masters call *mushin*—a mind that acts without clinging, like water flowing over stone.

Whenever you notice your chest tightening with irritation, anxiety, or lingering resentment about an earlier interaction, pause and ask yourself Tanzan’s question: 

*"Am I still carrying this?"*

Notice that the physical event is gone. The person who offended you is elsewhere, likely oblivious, eating their lunch or sleeping peacefully. The only place the conflict currently exists is inside the sanctuary of your own mind, maintained exclusively by your own attention.

To be free does not mean living without boundaries or principles. It means mastering the art of the clean completion: engage fully with whatever reality demands of you in the moment, do what must be done, and then leave it on the riverbank.
