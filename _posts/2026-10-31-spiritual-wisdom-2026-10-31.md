In the court of the Prince of Lu, there lived a woodcarver named Khing who fashioned bell-stands from wood. His work was so exquisitely beautiful, so seemingly organic, that people whispered it must have been carved by spirits. When the Prince asked Khing for the secret of his art, the woodcarver replied that he had no secret, only a method of preparation.

'When I am about to make a bell-stand,' Khing explained, 'I do not waste my energy. I first must fast in order to quiet my mind. When I have fasted for three days, I no longer think of rewards or money. After five days, I no longer think of praise or blame, of skill or clumsiness. After seven days, I have forgotten my own body and limbs. At that stage, my own self has ceased to exist for me. Only then do I enter the forest to look at the trees in their own natural state. When I see the right tree, the bell-stand appears before my eyes, and I simply apply my hand to the work.'

Khing’s process, known in Taoist philosophy as *xin zhai* or 'fasting of the mind,' reveals a profound truth about human excellence. He suggests that the greatest barrier to our work is not a lack of technique, but the accumulation of mental noise. By systematically stripping away the layers of the ego—financial anxiety, social validation, and even the self-conscious awareness of one's own identity—Khing allowed his internal 'heaven' (his natural essence) to meet the 'heaven' of the wood.

In our modern lives, we are often encouraged to do the exact opposite. We are told to focus on the 'bell-stand'—the promotion, the social media metrics, the final product—before we have even begun the work. This outcome-fixation creates a 'noisy' mind, filled with the static of comparison and the fear of failure. When we work from this state of agitation, we are forcing our will upon the world rather than collaborating with its natural flow.

To apply the wisdom of the woodcarver today is to practice selective mental silence. It means carving out spaces where the 'rewards' and 'praise' of the external world are intentionally ignored. Whether we are writing a report, raising a child, or solving a technical problem, the highest quality of action arises when we 'fast' from our own self-importance. When the ego is silenced, the work no longer feels like a struggle; it becomes a spontaneous expression of reality itself.
